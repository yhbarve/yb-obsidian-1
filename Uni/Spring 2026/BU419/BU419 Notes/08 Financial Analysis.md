# Lecture 08: Financial Analysis

### Introduction
- We will work through the various ratios by looking at various Canadian P&C companies.
- Using the ratios, we will answer questions about the financial health of these companies.
- And we'll also understand if the ratios present any warning signs for companies.
- - -
### Gross Written Premium / Operating DWP
- Gross Written Premium (GWP) and Operating Direct Written Premium (DWP) represent the total dollar amount of premiums written by an insurer during a specific period, before deducting any premiums ceded to reinsurance companies.
- This is the top-line revenue metric for an insurance business and reflects its market share and premium growth scale.
- When analyzing the metric, we are trying to understand the trajectory of the company's growth and the underlying forces shaping it.
	- The trend itself identifies whether the top-line premium volume is growing, stagnating, or declining.
	- We can also look at the breakdown by the lines of businesses and see if the growth is driven by a single booming product line or if it is balanced across the portfolio.
	- We can also connect the data to macro factors.
		- For example, if the premium volume increased because the insurer raised the premium rates due to high inflation or because of an economic boom, which increased the demand for commercial insurance.
	- We can also analyze if new regulations or intense competition forced companies to lower their premium prices.
- Remember the difference between written vs earned.
	- Written Premium represents the total value of all policies bound during that period, regardless of whether the cash has been collected. When a policy is written, the insurer secures the legal commitment for that revenue.
	- Earned premium, on the other hand, is the actual activity of the customer paying their premiums according to the contract or the insurance product purchased. It contributes to the actual cash earnings which happen over a period of time.
- Note the slight nuance between GWP and Operating DWP:
	- Direct written premium refers strictly to the policies the company issued directly to its own customers via its own agents or brokers.
	- Gross written premium, on the other hand, is a broader umbrella term.
		- $\text{GWP} = \text{DWP} + \text{Assumed Premium}$
		- Assumed premium is money the company makes by acting as a re-insurer.
	- Basically, if an insurer only writes policies for everyday customers and does not act as a re-insurer for other companies, its DWP and GWP are exactly the same number.

### Net Underwriting Revenue / Operating Net Underwriting Revenue
- Net underwriting revenue is the actual amount of premium money an insurance company gets to keep and recognizes as its revenue after paying for its safety net, which is reinsurance.
- $\text{Net Underwriting Revenue} = \text{Gross Premiums Earned} - \text{Premiums Paid to Reinsurers}$
- Net underwriting revenue includes everything related to premiums, even one-off accounting adjustments, oddities from discontinued product lines or unique regulatory payouts.
- On the other hand, operating net underwriting revenue strips away those one-offs and isolates the premium revenue generated strictly by the company's ongoing daily core insurance operations.
- Insurance companies treat premium as revenue because it is earned income for providing the service of taking on risk. The claims that may happen later are treated as expenses.
- ***If net underwriting revenue is significantly lower than GWP, it proves that a massive portion of the premiums and therefore the risk is being passed off to reinsurers.***
- - -
### Underwriting Income (UW)
- This is the profit made strictly from the insurance operations.
- $\text{Underwriting Income} = \text{Net Underwriting Revenue} - \text{Claims} - \text{Operating Expenses}$
- If this number is negative, it means that the company paid out more in claims and expenses than it kept in premiums.
- If the underwriting income is fluctuating, check if specific product lines are driving the losses or gains.
- - -
### Investment Income
- Insurance companies don't just let premium cash sit around while waiting for claims to be filed.
- They invest that cash into stocks, bonds, and real estate.
- This metric tracks the returns, dividends, and interest generated by that investment portfolio.
- When analyzing investment income, it is important to look at what their investment income is based on. Is the company heavily invested in safe, predictable government bonds, or are volatile market gains and losses swinging their bottom lines?
- - -
### Investment Income vs Underwriting Income
- It is common to analyze the proportion of underwriting income vs investment income.
- Some companies are highly efficient insurers, making solid profits from their policies, which is reflected by a high UW income.
- Other companies can be mediocre insurers that rely entirely on a booming stock market to stay profitable, which is reflected by a high investment income.
- - -
### Liquidity Measures
- Liquidity measures evaluate an insurer's immediate ability to pay out claims and short-term liabilities without needing to liquidate long-term investments at a loss.
- The two primary metrics are the cash ratio and the liabilities-to-liquid-assets ratio.
#### Cash Ratio
- $\text{Cash Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash}+\text{Investment Income Due}+\text{Short-Term Deposits}}{\text{Net Current Liabilities}}$
- It basically measures the dollars of pure cash and near-cash assets existing for every dollar of immediate short-term debt and obligations due within a year.
- A higher cash ratio means higher short-term safety.
- Investment income due refers to investment income like bond interest or stock dividends that the insurance company has legally earned but has not yet received in cash. It is an accounting receivable.
#### Liabilities to Liquid Assets Ratio
- It measures the proportion of total claims and obligations relative to assets that can be easily converted into cash within a short time frame.
- Lower the ratio, the safer it is, showing that liquid reserves easily cover potential obligations.
#### Cash Ratio vs Liabilities to Liquid Assets Ratio
| Cash Ratio                                                                    | Liabilities to Liquid Assets Ratio                                                                                                                                           |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Only counts pure cash or short-term deposits and investment income due.       | Uses a much broader definition of liquid assets, which includes things like stocks and longer-term bonds that can be sold quickly if needed, even if they aren't cash today. |
| Only looks at current liabilities, which are obligations due within one year. | Often looks at total insurance liabilities, including long-term claims that might take years to pay out.                                                                     |
