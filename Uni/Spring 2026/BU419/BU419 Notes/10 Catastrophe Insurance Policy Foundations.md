# Why Catastrophe Insurance is Different
- Catastrophe losses are increasing worldwide. This isn't just a Canadian problem. 
- Governments around the world are facing the same challenge of financing increasingly costly disasters.
## Drivers of Increasing Catastrophe Losses
1. **Hazard**
	- Increasing frequency and severity of secondary perils
	- Climate change alters the frequency, severity, and spatial distribution of weather-related perils.
	- Leads to more intense storms, prolonged wildfire seasons, and heavier precipitation events
	- Also leads to compounding events such as storm plus flooding or wildfire plus wind
2. **Vulnerability**
	- The replacement cost of structures, infrastructure, and technology escalates with time and as real wealth grows.
	- Most regions have inadequate flood and wildfire mitigation strategies.
	- With the rise of technology, new risk features like solar panels and batteries have been created.
3. **Exposure**
	- Population growth in high-risk areas creates denser pockets of value, turning what would have been a localized natural event into a multi-billion-dollar catastrophe.
	- It also means that there are simply more high-value physical assets sitting in harm's way.
4. **Socio-economic effects**
	- Post-disaster surges in demand for labour and raw materials, combined with general economic inflation, significantly push up the repair and rebuilding cost.
## Why catastrophe risks challenge private insurance markets?
1. **Losses are highly correlated**
	- A single event can generate thousands of claims simultaneously.
	- Risk cannot be diversified within a region. 
2. **Loss distributions are fat-tailed**
	- Rare events produce extremely large losses.
	- Extreme losses dominate total losses.
3. **Extreme losses require large capital**
	- Insurers must hold capital to survive rare but severe events.
	- Capital tied to low-probability risks is often expensive.
4. **Tail risk can threaten insurer solvency**
	- Tail risk refers to the possibility of extremely rare but exceptionally large losses.
	- Extreme catastrophe losses may exceed available industry capital.
	- Reinsurance capacity may be limited during large events.

# Designing Catastrophe Insurance Programs
## Catastrophe Insurance Programme Policy Goals
- **Affordability:** Insurance premiums remain within reach for households and businesses, including those in higher-risk areas.
- **Availability:** Coverage is widely offered so that most properties can obtain catastrophe insurance.
- **Equity/Fairness:** The distribution of costs and benefits across policyholders and taxpayers is viewed as fair.
- **Resilience:** this includes both economic recovery and the continued functioning and stability of insurance markets.
- **Reducing the protection gap:** A larger share of catastrophe losses is covered by insurance rather than borne by households or governments. Basically you want to minimize uninsured losses. **Protection Gap = Total Loss - Insured Loss**
- **Solvency:** insurers and the system remain able to pay claims after very large disasters.
- **Sustainability:** the program remains financially and politically viable over time.
## How does the nature of the parallel shape see a program design?
- How the insurance industry typically treats the peril?
	- Included in standard property policy
	- Covered through an endorsement
	- Or typically excluded. This usually involves government intervention to establish availability.
- Potential losses relative to available financial capacity of
	- The domestic economy
	- The primary insurance market
	- Global Reinsurance Capacity
- Return period of peak peril
	- How frequently catastrophic events occur?
	- How quickly must capital be replenished?
- Insurability  Trends
	- Climate trends may undermine long-term insurability.
## Six categories that structure CI programs
1. **Coverage Scope, and Participation**
	- This defines the exact boundary lines of the insurance program.
	- The peril scope could be single peril, which focuses on one specific hazard, or multi peril, which bundles multiple natural hazards together.
	- Programs decide whether to protect only residential homes or include commercial properties and public infrastructure. Expanding property types increases the total exposure but broadens the premium base.
	- **Coverage structure:** The policy could be either automatically bundled inside standard home and property policies or sold separately as an optional endorsement or add-on.
	- Participation Rules dictate who is required or permitted to operate within a CII program across three distinct levels:
		1. Insurer participation
			- If mandatory, all insurers writing standard property coverage in the market must cede risk to or participate in the public catastrophe pool. Ensures broad risk sharing and **avoids adverse selection.**
			- If voluntary, insurers choose whether to cede risk or use the program. 
		2. Coverage offer mandate
			- If mandatory, primary insurers are legally forced to offer catastrophe coverage to policyholders.
			- If voluntary, insurers can choose whether or not to make catastrophic coverage available. 
		3. Policy Holder Purchase Mandate
			- If mandatory property owners are legally required to buy the coverage.
			- If voluntary property owners decide whether to purchase the endorsement or the policy.
2. **Pricing & Cross Subsidization**
	- The key decision is to choose between the risk-based pricing versus cross-subsidization.
	- **Risk Based Pricing**
		- Under risk-based pricing, your premium reflects your exact expected loss plus reinsurance and regulatory capital costs.
		- A beachfront home or a house in a dense forest interface pays an extremely high premium, while a house on high ground or away from trees pays a low premium.
		- It sends clear financial warnings to property owners and deters people from building in high-risk flood plains or wildfire zones and awards mitigation.
		- It also ensures that the pool actually collects enough money to cover expected losses.
		- A downside is that premiums in extreme hazard zones can become very expensive, forcing people to drop the coverage, widening the protection gap.
	- **Cross-subsidization**
		- Under cross-subsidization, low-risk policyholders pay more than their actual risk to help cover the costs of high-risk policyholders.
		- Governments use four main levers to cross-subsidize:
			1. **Flat rate premiums:** Everyone pays the exact same flat rate or percentage, regardless of location.
			2. **Implicit cross-subsidies:** Averaging rates regionally or nationally, so lower-risk zones pad the fund for higher-risk zones
			3. **Capped Premiums:** Capping maximum rates in high-risk zones so homeowners aren't priced out
			4. **Explicit government subsidies:** Direct taxpayer cash injected into the pool to lower costs for policyholders
		- The core problem with cross-subsidies is that they create moral hazard. If living on a dangerous flood plain is heavily subsidized by urban condo owners, people have zero financial incentive to move out or reinforce their homes.
3. Capitalization & Funding
	- Initial Program Capitalization describes how the Catastrophe Insurance Program builds its starting balance sheet on day one.
		- **Government Capital or Disaster Funds:** Direct government grants, seed loans, or public disaster funds provide immediate capacity.
		- **Insurer contributions:** Primary insurance companies are required to contribute initial capital or equity to set up the pool.
		- **No initial fund:** The program starts with $0 in reserves and relies entirely on post-disaster borrowing, taxation, or industry assessments if an event occurs.
	- Program funding discusses how the program continuously finances losses across decades. There are three main funding approaches:
		1. Pre-funded
			- Premiums are collected from policyholders continuously and accumulate in our dedicated catastrophe reserve fund before a disaster strikes.
