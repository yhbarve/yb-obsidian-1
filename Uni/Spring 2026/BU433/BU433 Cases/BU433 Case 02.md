**Q2.**

**Q3.**
- **Climbing interest rates:** The Federal Reserve shifted its monetary policy and aggressively increased interest rates from 1% in June 2004 to 5.25% by July 2006.
- Bear Stearns' High Grade fund relied on the strategy of borrowing cash at low rates in the short-term to buy higher-yielding collateralized debt obligations (CDOs).
- When the interest rates climbed, the borrowing costs rose while the underlying CDO assets simultaneously collapsed in market value. This completely erased the High Grade fund's positive spread.
- As the spreads began to narrow, funds had to increase leverage to maintain the target return.

**Q4a.** 
- The hedge funds posted positive gains every month and a cumulative return of nearly 50% through January 2007, creating a false sense of security before collapsing into bankruptcy by July 2007.
- The underlying leverage ratio spiked dramatically in a matter of weeks, rising from 17.2 in January 2007 to 22.5 in February 2007, heavily amplifying the firm's vulnerability to market fluctuations.
- The vast majority of the funds' assets were locked in highly illiquid structures. As their market value plummeted, it forced them to liquidate massive portions of their funds at fire-sale prices just to satisfy escalating creditor margin calls.
- This problem was not isolated. Other financial institutions were also facing similar issues.

**Q4b.**
- Initially, the management operated under a highly speculative trading philosophy: 'Easy come, easy go'.
- As their model collapsed, a crucial step was taken in 2008 to convert surviving independent entities into traditional bank holding companies (holdcos). This decision effectively capped their gross leverage ratio at 10x.
- An alternative option could have been to proactively restrict their high gross leverage, scale back their reliance on unstable short-term repo lines, and adjust to a lower-return profile earlier to insulate their capital base.

**Q4c.**
- Management's response was entirely inadequate because their structural reliance on a massive mismatch in maturity directly violates the most basic premise in risk management.
- Instead of scaling back their highly leveraged balance sheet when early warnings arose, they relied on manufactured ratings from investment banks to mask credit quality deterioration and continued to dance on a fragile foundation of overnight debt.
