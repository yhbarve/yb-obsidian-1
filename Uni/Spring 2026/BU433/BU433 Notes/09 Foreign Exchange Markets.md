### Overview of Foreign Exchange Markets
- Cash flows from the sale of products, services, or assets denominated in a foreign currency are part of the foreign exchange markets.
- A foreign exchange rate is the price at which one currency can be exchanged for another currency in the foreign exchange markets.
- Currency depreciation occurs when a currency becomes less valuable relative to another currency.
- Appreciation occurs when a currency is more valuable relative to another currency.
#### Short View Back To The Past
- 1800s: Gold Standard (currencies backed by gold)
- 1944-1971: Bretton Woods (fixed exchange rates with government intervention)
- 1971 - 1973: Smithsonian Agreements (devalued the US Dollar, broke the bands, and created the free-floating system)
- 1999: Introduction of the Euro
- An analogy:
	- **The Gold Era:** Back in the day, everyone agreed that 1 Blue Ticket was worth exactly 1 piece of gold candy. Simple.
	- **The Strict Rule Era (Bretton Woods):** Later, the adults made a rule: "1 Blue Ticket is always worth exactly 2 Red Tickets. No changing it!"
	- **The Free Era (Today):** Eventually, the adults gave up and said, "Fine, decide yourself." Now, if everyone wants Blue Tickets to buy a cool toy, the price goes up. If nobody wants them, the price goes down.
- Euro is the European Union's single currency, which started trading on January 1, 1999.
- Dollar still remains the unparalleled medium of exchange because it is the world's easiest currency to buy or sell.
	- The US economy is well trusted and countries have faith that the US dollar won't lose its value in the next few years.
	- The world's price tag is measured in US dollars. All the major trades happen in US dollars, which is why it's easy for the world to embrace it as the base currency.
- Dollarization is the use of a foreign currency either in parallel or completely instead of the local currency.
	- This could occur officially or unofficially.
	- For example countries like Ecuador and Panama completely got rid of their local currency and started using U.S. dollars for daily life activities.

### Foreign Exchange Rates
-  FX rates are listed in two ways: direct and indirect quotes
	- Direct Quotes: US Dollars received for one unit of foreign currency
	- Indirect Quotes: Foreign currency received for each US Dollar
- There are two types of FX transactions: Spot & Forward:
	- Spot transactions involve the immediate exchange of currencies at the current or spot exchange rate.
		- There's the risk of the value of the foreign currency changing relative to the US dollar over the holding period 
	- Forward transaction is the exchange of currencies at a specified exchange rate (or forward exchange) rate at some specified date in the future.
- Of the average daily trading volume, 28% is involved in spot transactions while 72% is involved in forward and other types of transactions.
- Future contracts are usually used for hedging, which means protecting yourself from a potential loss.
- Hedging is usually done by entering into a second contract of opposite parity, which is known as closing out your position.

### Role of Financial Institutions in FX Transactions
- FX market transactions are conducted among dealers mainly OTC using telecommunication and computer networks.
- A financial institution's net exposure is the overall FX exposure in any given currency, measured by its net book or position exposure. $$ Net\ Exposure_i = (FX\ assets_i - FX\ liabilities_i) + (FX\ bought_i = FX\ sold_i)$$
-  A positive net exposure implies that an FI is overall net long in a currency. A negative net exposure position implies that the FI is net short in a foreign currency.
- A financial institution's position generally reflects four trading activities:
	- Purchase and sale of foreign currencies to allow customers to complete international commercial trade transactions.
	- Purchase and sale of foreign currencies to allow customers or the FI itself to take positions in foreign real and financial investments.
	- Purchase and sale of foreign currencies for hedging purposes to offset customer or FII exposure in any given currency.
	- Purchase and sale of foreign currencies for speculative purposes through forecasting or anticipating future movements in FX rates.

### Interaction of Interest Rates, Inflation, and Exchange Rates
#### Fisher Effect
- $i = IP + RFR$
	- $i =$ Interest rate
	- $IP =$ Inflation rate
	- $RFR =$ Real risk-free rate
- The international Fisher effect incorporated affects the rates in the relationship. $$E(S_{US$/C$}) = S_{US$/C$} * \frac{1 + i_{US}}{1 + i_C}$$
#### Purchasing Power Parity
- It is the theory explaining the change in foreign currency exchange rates as inflation rates in the countries change.
- The PPP theorem states that the change in the exchange rate between two countries' currencies is proportional to the difference in their inflation rates. $$IP_{US} - IP_{S} = \frac{\Delta S_{US/S}}{S_{US/S}}$$
#### Interest Rate Parity Theorem
- Interest rate parity theorem is the theory that the domestic interest rate should equal the foreign interest rate minus the expected appreciation of the domestic currency.
