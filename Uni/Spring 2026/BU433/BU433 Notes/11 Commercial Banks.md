# Ch 11: Commercial Banks - Industry Overview
---
### Commercial Banks
- Commercial banks are the largest group of financial institutions in terms of the dollar value of assets.
- Major assets are loans and major liabilities are deposits.
	- For a commercial bank the deposits from its customers are liabilities because that's the money it owes to its customers. On the other hand the loans that the bank provides are assets because that is money that is owed to the bank.
- Investment securities generate interest income and provide banks with liquidity.
- Commercial banks face unique risks because of their asset structure:
	- **Credit or default risk** is the risk that promised cash flows from loans and securities held by FIs may not be paid in full.
	- **Liquidity risk** is the risk that a sudden and unexpected increase in liability withdrawals may require a financial institution to liquidate assets in a very short period of time and at very low prices.
	- **Interest rate risk** is the risk incurred by an FI when the maturities of its assets and liabilities are mismatched and interest rates are volatile.
	- The above risks all contribute to a commercial bank's level of **insolvency risk**, which is the risk that an FI may not have enough capital to offset a sudden decline in the value of its assets relative to its liabilities.
---
### Commercial Bank Liabilities
#### Deposits
- Transaction accounts are checkable deposits that are either demand deposits or negotiable order of withdrawal (NOW) accounts.
- Household Savings and Time Deposits
- Large time deposits ($100,000 or more) 
#### Borrowed or Other Liability Funds
- Non-deposit liabilities include a broad area of instruments like federal funds, repurchase agreements, notes and bonds.
- Relatively more liquid instruments such as deposits and interbank borrowings are used to fund relatively less liquid assets such as loans.
#### Equity
- Commercial bank equity capital consists mainly of:
	1. Common and preferred stock,
	2. Surplus or additional paid-in capital,
	3. Retained earnings
- Largest banks are subject to annual stress tests, which are designed to ensure that the banks are properly capitalized.
---
### Off-Balance-Sheet (OBS) Activities
- These are many fee-related activities that banks engage in off the balance sheet.
	- Issuing various types of guarantees, such as letters of credit
	- Making future commitments to land
	- Engaging in derivative transactions such as futures, forwards, options, and swaps
- Losses from the falling value of OBS securities were the major reason for the financial crisis in 2009.
- Other fee-generating activities include:
	- Trust services offered only by the largest banks
		- Holds and manages assets for individuals or corporations 
	- Correspondent banking (providing banking services to other banks that do not have the staff resources to perform the services themselves)
---
### Shadow Banking
- These are activities of non-financial firms that perform banking services.
- Because these are not traditional banks, they face significantly reduced regulation.
- Even so, because there is less regulation, there is a greater risk that something can go wrong. If it does, it will not only affect these nonbank firms but also the overall banking system. 
---
### Bank Size & Concentration
- Community banks have less than $1 billion in asset size and tend to specialize in retail banking.
	- Retail banking is consumer-oriented banking, such as providing residential and consumer loans or accepting small deposits.
- Relative asset share of largest banks has increased from 63.4% in 1984 to 95.1% in 2021.
	- Mergers & Acquisitions, and expansion of services to new domains has caused this.
- Small banks tend to focus on the retail side:
	- Generally hold fewer OBS assets and liabilities than large banks.
	- Rarely hold derivative securities.
	- More sheltered from competition in highly localized markets.
	- Lend to smaller, less sophisticated customers than large banks.
- Large banks usually engage in both retail and wholesale banking but focus on the wholesale side of the business:
	- Operate with lower amounts of equity capital than small banks
	- Tend to use more purchased funds and have fewer cold deposits
	- Pay higher salaries and invest more in buildings and premises
	- Partake in more OBS activities and therefore generate more non-interest income than small banks

### Industry Performance
- Early 2000s Prosperity:
	- As the Federal Reserve cut interest rates 13 times, owning a home became highly affordable.
	- Advances in IT and new financial instruments allowed banks to manage risks more effectively and shift credit risks off their balance sheets.
- Peak before the crash:
	- Even when rising interest rates caused a mild decline, performance remained historically high. In 2006, capital ratios hit a multi-decade high.
- Late 2000s severe collapse:
	- Performance plummeted as the Great Recession hit.
	- Net income dropped by 31%, and average return on assets fell below 1%.
	- By 2008, 25% of banking institutions were completely unprofitable.
- Recovery & Pandemic resilience:
	- The industry steadily recovered, with ROE rising from 0.65% to 1.04%.
	- By the end of 2019, 96.4% of banks were profitable.
	- After COVID, the industry fully rebounded to the pre-pandemic levels by 2021.

### Regulators
1. **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)** insures deposits of commercial banks.
2. **Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)** is the oldest US bank regulatory agency.
3. **Federal Reserve System (FRS)** serves as the country's central bank and has regulatory power over some banks.
4. **State bank regulators** perform functions similar to those the OCC performs, but only for state-chartered commercial banks

### Global Issues
- Benefits of international expansion
	- Risk diversification
	- Economies of Scale
	- Innovations
	- Funds Source
	- Customer Relationships
	- Regulatory Avoidance
- Drawbacks of international expansion
	- Information and monitoring costs
	- Nationalization
	- Other fixed costs
- Because modern mega-banks operate seamlessly across borders, they create tightly interconnected global networks. When a crisis hits a primary market (the collapse of the U.S. mortgage market in 2008) the shockwaves travel instantly through these international banking channels.
