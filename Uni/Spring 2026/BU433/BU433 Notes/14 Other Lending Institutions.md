### Trust Companies
- Trust companies are depository institutions that also serve as a corporate trustee with two distinct roles:
	- Intermediation function by accepting deposits and making loans
		- Just like a regular bank, they take people's money to keep it safe and then lend that money out to other people who want to buy houses.
	- Fiduciary function by providing estates, trusts, and agencies business
		- This is the special superpower. Sometimes people have a lot of money, a big estate, or a business, but they do not want to manage it themselves.
		- In such cases, a trust company steps in as a legal manager to protect those assets for them, even though the company does not own the money.
		-  even though they are in control of the money, they cannot use it for other purposes. It still stays secure in a private box.
		- Trust companies take on this responsibility for the steady, guaranteed fees and commissions, which is known as fee-based income. It’s very popular because it’s incredibly safe and predictable, and companies love it.
- Most trust companies are subsidiaries of banks and insurers. Independent ones make up only a small portion.
- Clients who do not want to manage their day-to-day finances benefit from using a trust company as one-stop shopping.

### Mortgage Loan Companies
- Banks were not allowed to offer residential mortgages. Therefore, mortgage loan companies felt the need.
- MLCs and trust companies are collectively known as trust and loan companies (TMLs), one of the four pillars of the Canadian financial services industry up to the mid-1980s.
- TMLs take deposits and primarily make residential mortgage loans, but can also make loans for commercial properties.
- TML's fund their assets with checkable and savings deposits, term deposits, GICs, basically borrow short and lend long.
- This strategy easily exposes TMLs to interest rate risks and liquidity risks.
- To properly address these issues, TMLs must have:
	1. Adequate liquid assets
	2. A contingency plan in place for funding requirements
	3. A timely statement of risk assessment results to be reported to OSFI

### Credit Unions & Caisses Populaires
#### Overview
- Credit unions and Caisses Populaires are the two cooperative banks that focus on servicing their members only.
- These are local alternatives to savings and borrowing institutions.
- A credit union can be established by a small group of members, with each member contributing a minimum share capital of $5 to $25.
- Traditionally, membership is based on a shared common bond, such as working in the same industry, belonging to the same trade union, or living in the same community.
- This common-bond requirement has limited the growth of these institutions.
#### Industry Composition
- Caisses populaires are centered in Quebec, while credit unions appear in the rest of Canada.
- Currently, there are 241 credit unions and caisses populaires outside Quebec and 271 under the Desjardins group in Quebec.
- The importance of these institutions varies from province to province.
#### Pros
- Earn higher interest rates than big banks, but not as high as Tangerine and EQ Bank
- Pay low transaction fees
- Get discounted rates for personal loans and mortgages
- Have superb customer service with personalized attention
- Stronger support from local entities for community developments
#### Cons
- Common bond membership is required, which typically involves a cost
- Few financial products offered
- Limited access with fewer branch locations
- May lack in terms of technology
- Benefits may not be shared equally by members
#### Industry Performance
- Profits are not necessarily their primary goal.
- With simple financial structures and democratic management styles, they just need to have sufficient capital to cover financial and operational risks
- They make money from transaction fees, interest on loans, mortgages, and credit cards paid by their members
- Although they will likely remain small compared to other financial institutions, their cost advantages have contributed towards their growth and popularity
- Credit unions concentrate more on providing their members with consumer loans that are funded by members' shares rather than deposits
- Caisses populaires emphasizes residential mortgage lending and deposit taking, encouraging the habit of savings
#### Organizational Structure
- A three-tier structure is formed by local credit unions, provincial credit union centrals, and the Canadian Credit Union Association (CCUA).
- The system is slightly different in Quebec but also includes three tiers
	- Local caisses populaires are organized into 11 federations belonging to the Desjardins Group.
	- Besides acting as the central bank, the Desjardins Group serves as a regulator for system stability.
#### Regulation
- Most credit unions are regulated at the provincial or territorial level.
- Each province has legislation to define the liquidity for a credit union to maintain.
- Regulators are responsible for ensuring that these institutions follow sound financial practices and submit annual financial statements with external auditors.
- Credit unions are not directly covered by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation.
- Instead, each provincial or territorial government has an agency providing deposit guarantees.
	- In Ontario, the Financial Services Regulatory Authority oversees the functioning of Credit Unions and protects depositors from loss of their deposits up to $250,000.
	- Deposits in registered accounts are insured with no maximum.

### Finance Companies
#### Overview
- Finance companies are now known as non-depository credit intermediaries in Canada.
- Just like banks, they offer lending services such as mortgages, loans, equipment leasing, and credit cards, but have no depository function.
- They act as intermediaries in the money market by borrowing in large amounts but often lending in smaller amounts, unlike banks.
- They lend to many of the same customers as banks, but they are virtually unregulated.
#### Types of Finance Companies
- Sales finance institutions specialize in loans to customers of a particular retailer or manufacturer
- Personal credit institutions specialize in instalments and other loans to consumers.
	- Make loans to riskier customers at higher interest rates than banks.
- Business credit institutions specialize in business loans, especially through equipment leasing and factoring
	- Not subject to regulations that restrict the type of products and services they can offer
	- Do not accept deposits.
	- More willing to accept risky customers 
	- Generally have lower overhead than banks 
#### Regulation
- Not subject to the Bank Act or CDIC, as they do not accept deposits. Much lower regulatory burden than depository institutions.
- Even with less regulatory scrutiny, finance companies must signal safety and soundness to capital markets in order to obtain funds.
#### Industry Performance
- Strong loan demand
- Strong profits for the largest firms
	- HSBC Finance, Associates First Capital, Beneficial
- Most successful finance companies have become takeover targets
	- Citigroup: merged with Associates First Capital
	- CIT: merged with Laurentian Capital
- Primary asset of a typical finance company is the loan portfolio:
	- Consumer loans: 58.5%
	- Business loans: 28.7%
	- Mortgage: 12.8%
- Liabilities (sources of funds):
	1. Corporate papers having short-term maturities of 30 days or less.
	2. Debt consisting of long-term notes and bonds.
- Finance companies rely heavily on issued corporate papers: short-term, unsecured promissory notes sold by higher-credit-quality corporations.
- They also have bank lines of credit as a backup
#### Global Issues
- Largest finance companies are in the US. Most Canadian finance companies are subsidiaries of US companies.
- In foreign countries, finance companies are generally subsidiaries of commercial banks or industrials.
