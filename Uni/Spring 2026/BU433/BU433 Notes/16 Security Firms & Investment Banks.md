# Ch 16: Security Firms & Investment Banks
---
### Securities Firms vs Investment Banks
#### Core Difference
- Securities firms and investment banks help net suppliers of funds (e.g., households) transfer funds to net users of funds (e.g., businesses) at a low cost and with maximum efficiency.
- Investment banking involves transactions such as the raising of debt and equity securities for corporations or governments.
	- In simple terms, they connect individuals with excess money to businesses that require money to expand.
	- Once connected, they create debt and equity instruments for transferring money.
		- Since they issue these instruments, they operate in the primary market.
		- Securities firms, on the other hand, deal with existing instruments and operate in the secondary market 
- Securities services involve assistance in the trading of securities in the secondary markets.
	- Securities firms specialize in the purchase, sale, and brokerage of existing securities
#### Industry Size & Composition
- The size of the industry is traditionally measured by the equity capital of participating firms, rather than by their total "asset size".
- The number of firms has fluctuated dramatically over time, heavily driven by regulatory changes
	- 1980: 5,248 firms
	- 1987: 9,515 firms
	- 2021: 3,794 firms
- The industry was irrevocably changed by the subprime collapse. The five largest investment banks at the start of 2008 were completely gone as independent investment banks by the end of that year.
#### Subgroups of Industry Firms
- National Full-Service Investment Banks are the largest diversified financial service firms that assist both retail customers (as broker-dealers) and corporate customers (via underwriting).
	- Commercial Bank Holding Companies: The absolute largest institutional players with massive domestic and international operations (e.g., JPMorgan).
	- Corporate Finance Specialists: National firms highly concentrated on primary market underwriting and secondary market trading (e.g., Goldman Sachs).
	- Institutional Specialists: Large investment banks that maintain limited branch networks outside of major cities, focusing predominantly on institutional clients (e.g., Greenhill & Co.).
- Specialized and Regional Firms target a specific segment of the financial markets rather than trying to do everything
	- Regional Securities Firms: Provide a mix of primary and secondary services for a specific geographic region (e.g., Raymond James Financial).
	- Specialized Discount Brokers: Focus strictly on low-cost retail transaction execution (e.g., Charles Schwab).
	- Specialized Electronic Trading Firms: Handle direct-access internet-based trading floor execution (e.g., E*Trade).
	- Venture Capital (VC) & Private Equity (PE) Firms: Focus on direct equity investments into private companies.
	- Boutiques: Highly specialized research or niche advisory firms.

### Investment Banking
- Refers specifically to the activities surrounding the underwriting and distribution of new debt and equity securities.
	- New issues can be either first-time issues (IPOs) or new issues of a firm whose debt or equity is already trading.
- It is a massive global business, with $11.47 trillion of debt and equity underwritten globally in 2021.
- Two Primary Distribution Routes:
	- Public Offerings: Securities are sold to the public at large. These can be structured on a firm commitment basis (the bank buys the whole batch and takes the risk) or a best efforts basis (the bank acts as an agent without guaranteeing the sale).
	- Private Offerings (Placements): The investment bank bypasses the public market entirely, acting as an agent to place the securities directly with one or a few large institutional investors (like a life insurance company) in exchange for a fee.
---
### Venture Capital & Private Equity
- **Venture Capital (VC)** is a professionally managed pool of money used to finance new, untried, and often high-risk startup firms that struggle to get traditional bank loans because they lack assets or business history.
	- VC firms are rarely passive; they provide deep management expertise, help with executive recruiting, and leverage professional business/scientific teams to identify emerging technologies.
	- Institutional VC firms have limited partner, financial, or corporate entities.
	- Angels are wealthy individuals making direct equity investments.
- **Private Equity (PE)** deals with **existing companies** that have already proven themselves in the field.
	- Looks at established operations, often focusing on restructuring or scaling proven entities.
	- Focuses more on traditional corporate financial evaluation and operational execution.
- As a result of the financial crisis, the differences between VC and PE firms have become less distinct.
---
### Market Making
- Market making involves the creation of a secondary market in an asset by a securities firm or investment bank to ensure liquidity.
- **Agency Transactions**: Two-way transactions executed directly on behalf of customers.
- **Principal Transactions**: The market maker uses its own balance sheet to take long or short inventory positions, seeking to profit directly from price movements.
- **Designated Market Makers (DMMs)**: Specialized roles on the NYSE that provide liquidity by assuming risk and displaying quotes in the exchange limit order book (accounting for about 17% of liquidity-adding volume in 2019).
	- Designated market makers are required to step in and buy equity or other securities even when the market is crashing, because their job is to maintain a fair and orderly market on the exchange.
	- They have ways to protect themselves and survive even in really bad situations.
		- If a wave of selling hits the market, the market maker will quickly lower the price they are willing to pay. They may still buy your shares, but they will do it at a steep discount to protect themselves from the falling market. In a panic, the risk of holding inventory skyrockets.
		- To compensate, market makers widen the gap between the bid and ask. Instead of a $0.01 spread, they might use a $0.50 spread, which gives them a much larger profit margin if they manage to flip the security.
		- Principal transactions can involve taking long or short inventory positions. If they expect the market to fall, they can use short positions or derivatives to hedge their risk and offset the losses from the inventory they are being forced to buy.

### Trading
- Traders take an **active net position** in an underlying asset, betting on price direction or market inefficiencies.
- Six distinct types of trading activities:
	- Position Trading: Involves purchasing large blocks of securities based on the expectation of a favorable, long-term price move.
	- Pure Arbitrage: Involves purchasing large blocks of securities based on the expectation of a favorable, long-term price move.
	- Risk Arbitrage: Buying securities in anticipation of a major information release, such as a merger or takeover announcement.
	- Program Trading: Using computer programs to simultaneously buy and sell a portfolio of at least 15 different stocks valued at more than $1 million total.
	- Stock Brokerage: Trading securities on behalf of customers as an agent, rather than taking a personal position.
	- Electronic Brokerage: Direct access to the trading floor via the internet, bypassing traditional human brokers entirely.
---
### Investing & Cash Management
- Investing involves managing massive pools of assets, such as closed-end and open-end mutual funds.
	- Securities firms can manage their funds either as agents (for other investors) or as principals (for themselves and their own stockholders)
	- The main objective is to select strategic asset portfolios designed to beat a specific benchmark, such as the S&P 500 index.
- Securities firms and investment banks offer bank deposit-like cash management accounts.
	- When a customer sells stock, the money goes into their brokerage account (also called the cash management account). Instead of letting that cash sit idle, the firm automatically invests it in a money market mutual fund so it can earn a bit of interest.
	- The client’s money remains fully liquid and can be withdrawn whenever they want.
---
### Other Service Functions
- Investment banks frequently provide advice and assistance related to mergers and acquisitions.
	- Assist in finding merger partners.
	- Underwrite any new securities to be issued by the merged firms.
	- Assess the value of the target firms.
	- Recommend terms of the merger agreement.
	- Assist the target firms in preventing a merger.
- Beyond trading and underwriting, investment banks handle essential back-office and administrative functions for a fee.
	- Holding and protecting client assets and funds securely
	- Managing backend paperwork, payments, and transfer of ownership when trades occur 
	- Providing deep market analysis and stock recommendations to clients 
		- Portion of the fee or commission allocated to research and advisory services is called soft dollars
---
### Recent Trends
- Industry profitability and commission income are highly cyclical, tied closely to the health of the stock market and the broader economy.
	- Commission income recovered strongly during the 1990s and mid-2000s economic expansions.
	- Revenues crashed during the 2006–2008 subprime mortgage collapse and rising oil prices.
- Tightened post-crisis regulations focused heavily on risk-taking and stricter capital requirements.
- Pretax profits initially dipped under early regulation (2013–2015), but rose through 2021 as revenues began outpacing expenses.
---
### Assets
- Many assets on the balance sheet are highly liquid, cash-like money market instruments rather than long-term capital positions.
	- **Receivables (30.7%)**: Owed from other broker-dealers and clearing organizations.
	- **Reverse Repos (28.7%)**: Short-term collateralized lending agreements.
	- **Long Positions (13.3%)**: Actual securities held in stock/debt inventory.
- Most assets are highly liquid because their own equity capital is very less. They need to have other people's money liquid in case they need to return it back.
---
### Liabilities
- The industry relies on extreme financial leverage funded by very short-term, market-based debt.
- Securities firms tend to use extremely short-term, market-based liabilities.
- Repurchase agreements are a major source of funds, accounting for about 36% of total liabilities.
- Other significant sources of funds include loans and payables to other broker-dealers and clearing organizations.
- Equity capital is only 6.8% of total assets, therefore it needs to maintain its assets in a highly liquid manner to avoid illiquidity risk.
---
### Regulation
- Securities & Exchange Commission (SEC) is the primary regulator
	- The **National Securities Markets Improvement Act (1996)** legally solidified the SEC’s supreme role
- While the SEC sets the broad laws, the **Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA)** handles daily regulation of trading practices.
	- It is an independent, non-profit organization authorized by Congress (not a government agency).
- The **Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC)** protects investor accounts up to specific limits if a brokerage firm physically fails.
	- Losses of up to $500,000 on securities firm failures
- U.S. Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations was created with the broad mandate to determine whether any changes are required in U.S. law to better protect the public.
---
### Global Issues
- Securities firms and investment banks operate heavily across borders, making their revenues deeply sensitive to global equity markets, international interest rate shocks, and geopolitical events.
- Ongoing monitoring of European markets remains critical, as historical sovereign debt expansions and localized financial crises in Europe create severe systemic risk and liquidity constraints worldwide.
- Growth strategies heavily focus on expanding underwriting, advisory, and asset management functions into rapidly growing emerging economies.
- Operating in multiple jurisdictions forces firms to navigate varying legal frameworks.
