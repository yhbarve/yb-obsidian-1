# Ch 17: Investment Companies
---
### Investment Companies
- Financial Institutions (FIs) that pool the financial resources of individuals and companies and invest those resources in diversified portfolios of assets.
- Two primary vehicles:
	- Open-end Mutual Funds: These funds sell new shares to investors and redeem outstanding shares on demand at their fair market values.
	- Hedge Funds (HFs): This type of investment pool solicits funds from wealthy individuals and other institutional investors (like commercial banks) and invests the money on their behalf. Unlike mutual funds, access here is strictly restricted to a more affluent client base.
---
### Mutual Funds
#### Industry Overview
- The first mutual fund was established in Boston in 1924, growing slowly at first.
- However, the industry exploded in scale due to a few catalysts:
	- The Advent of Money Market Mutual Funds (MMMFs): Launched in 1972 to invest in short-term securities (maturities under one year), followed by tax-exempt MMMFs in 1979.
	- Special-Purpose Funds: An explosion of specialized equity, bond, emerging market, and derivative funds.
	- The Rise of Retirement Funds: A massive influx of institutional funds managing employer-sponsored retirement plans. This includes highly popular target date funds (which automatically reallocate and reduce risk over time) and lifestyle funds (which maintain a fixed, predetermined risk level).
- The mutual fund industry is larger than the insurance industry but only slightly larger than the depository institutions industry.
- Low barriers to entry in the U.S. mutual fund industry have allowed new entrants to offer funds to compete for investor attention
#### Types of Mutual Funds
- Short-term funds comprise taxable money market mutual funds and tax-exempt money market funds.
	- Invest in short-term fixed income securities that often mature in under 60 days.
	- The main risk is interest rate risk, which is highly mitigated by short maturities
	- These have virtually no liquidity or default risks.
- Long-term funds comprise equity funds, bond funds, and hybrid funds.
	- Equity funds consist of common and preferred stock securities. These are typically well diversified, and the risk is more systematic or market based.
	- Bond Funds consist of fixed income capital market debt securities. There is an extensive interest rate risk because of their long-term fixed-rate nature.
	- Hybrid funds consist of both stock and bond securities.
- Households own the majority of both long- and short-term funds. As of 2021, 47.9% of households owned mutual funds.
---
#### Other Types of Investment Company Funds
- Open-end mutual funds are a fund for which the supply of shares is not fixed but can increase or decrease daily with purchases and redemptions of shares.
- A closed-end investment company is a specialized investment company that has a fixed supply of outstanding shares and generally does not continuously offer shares for sale.
	- Real estate investment trusts are closed-end investment companies that specialize in investing in real estate company shares and/or in buying mortgages.
- Unit investment trusts sell a fixed number of redeemable shares that are redeemed on a set termination date.
	- The Termination Date: Every UIT has a built-in expiration date (e.g., exactly 5 years from launch). When that date arrives, the trust automatically shuts down, sells all the underlying assets for cash, and automatically pays every investor their fair share of the profits.
	- Redeemable Shares: You don't _have_ to wait for that final date. If you need your money early, the shares are "redeemable," meaning you can hand them back to the trust at any time, and they will buy them back from you at their current mathematical value.
---
#### Index Funds & Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs)
- Index funds are funds in which managers buy securities in proportions similar to those included in a specified major index.
	- Index funds involve very little research or management, which results in lower fees and higher returns than more actively managed funds.
- Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) are long-term mutual funds that are also designed to replicate a particular stock market index.
	- Even though they are open-ended under the hood, they trade on a secondary stock exchange just like a regular stock. This gives them structural advantages over index funds.
	- An ETF is essentially an index fund that _does_ trade on a secondary market. If you own an ETF tracking the S&P 500, you don't go back to the fund company to cash out; you jump onto a stock exchange at 11:00 AM and sell your share directly to another investor, trading instantly at real-time market prices.
---
#### Investment Returns from Mutual Fund Ownership
- Total return comes from three distinct aspects of the underlying portfolio:
	- Income and Dividends: The fund passes along the regular interest earned on bonds and the dividends paid out by the stocks it holds
	- Capital Gains Distributions: When the fund manager sells an asset inside the portfolio for a higher price than they bought it for, those realized profits are distributed to you.
	- Capital Appreciation: The remaining assets inside the fund increase in market value, which directly pushes up the value of all the outstanding shares in the mutual fund.
- Net asset value (NAV) of a mutual fund share is equal to the market value of the assets in the mutual fund portfolio divided by the number of shares outstanding.
---
#### Mutual Fund Costs
- Sales Loads (One-Time Fees):
    - Load Fund: Charges an upfront or one-time sales commission. Proponents argue it pays for personal attention and advice.
    - No-Load Fund: Does not charge any upfront sales or commission fees.
- Fund Operating Expenses (Annual Fees):
    - Management Fee: Charged to meet regular administration and shareholder service operating costs. It is generally the largest fee an owner faces.
    - 12b-1 Fees: Annual fees tied to the marketing and distribution costs of the fund shares. Under FINRA rules, the marketing/distribution portion cannot exceed 0.75% of average net assets per year.
- Often, funds offer different share classes, but each share class may offer investors different methods of paying for broker services.
	- Class A shares carry a front-end load that is charged at the time of purchase.
	- Class B shares are offered for sale at NAV without a front-end load but charge an annual 12b-1 fee and a back-end load
	- Class C shares are offered at NAV with no front-end load but charge an annual 12b-1 fee of 1% and a back-end load (1% if sold in the first year, otherwise 0)

#### Mutual Fund Regulations
- Mutual funds are heavily regulated because they manage and invest small investor savings.
- SEC is the primary regulator.
	- Securities Act of 1933: Requires mutual funds to file a formal registration statement with the SEC and establishes strict rules regarding what must go into the fund's prospectus.
	- Securities Exchange Act of 1934: Makes the buying and selling of all mutual fund shares subject to various strict anti-fraud provisions.
	- Investment Company Act & Investment Advisers Act of 1940: Passed by Congress to establish the fundamental operating boundaries and fiduciary duties for investment companies and their managers.
	- Insider Trading and Securities Fraud Enforcement Act of 1988: Required mutual funds to build explicit internal mechanisms and procedures to completely block insider trading abuses.
	- Market Reform Act of 1990: Grants the SEC the power to introduce "circuit breakers" to completely halt exchange trading and restrict program trading during times of extreme market volatility.
	- National Securities Market Improvement Act (NSMIA) of 1996: Streamlined the system by exempting mutual fund companies from oversight by state-level regulators, leaving federal regulation in charge.
- Several allegations of trading abuse and improper assignment of fees were revealed and prosecuted in the early 2000s.
	- Market Timing: Short-term trading of mutual fund shares to exploit time-zone or pricing discrepancies between stale portfolio values and real-time market data.
	- Late Trading: A highly illegal practice where favored investors were allowed to buy or sell mutual fund shares _after_ the official 4:00 PM EST daily price had already been locked in.
	- Directed Brokerage: When brokers improperly allowed their recommendations to be influenced by mutual fund companies, rather than acting in the best interest of the investor.
	- Improperly Assessed Fees: Brokers tricking retail customers into believing they were buying no-load funds, or intentionally failing to pass along standard volume discounts.
- To put a stop to these abuses, the SEC enacted a series of aggressive rules starting in late 2004.
	- Chief Compliance Officers: Every mutual fund is now legally required to hire a dedicated Chief Compliance Officer to monitor internal operations.
	- Mandatory Fair Value Pricing: Funds must actively adjust stale stock prices to ensure market timers cannot extract risk-free profits at the expense of long-term shareholders.
	- The 4:00 PM Hard Close: To permanently kill late trading, a proposal was made requiring all trading orders to be physically received by the fund or its designated agent by exactly 4:00 PM Eastern.
	- Radical Fee Transparency: Shareholder reports must explicitly break down the exact fees paid out of investor accounts, and funds must provide absolute clarity regarding brokerage commissions and available discounts.

### Hedge Funds
- Hedge funds are investment pools that invest funds for wealthy individuals and other investors like commercial banks.
	- Historically, investors needed to have a net worth of over $1 million or an annual income of over $200,000.
	- Because they avoid heavy consumer regulation, managers use highly aggressive strategies that are banned for mutual funds, including massive leverage, short selling, arbitrage, and complex derivatives.
- Since HFs that do not exceed 100 million in assets under management do not register with the SEC, their actual data cannot be independently tracked. The data is self-reported.
- There are three types of hedge funds:
	- Market directional funds are more risky and seek high returns using leverage, typically investing based on anticipated events.
	- Market neutral or value orientation funds have moderate exposure to risk, typically favoring a longer-term investment strategy
	- Market neutral funds strive for moderate, consistent returns with low risk.
- Management fees are computed as a percentage of the total assets under management.
- Performance fees are unique to hedge funds, and the average performance fee is approximately 20%, but varies widely.
	- A hurdle rate is a minimum annualized performance benchmark that must be met before a performance fee can be assessed.
	- A high-water mark is typically used by hedge funds where the manager does not receive a performance fee unless the fund’s net asset value exceeds its highest value it has previously achieved.
- While mutual funds are heavily regulated, hedge funds have generally been unregulated.
	- They operate under two main exemptions from the Investment Company Act of 1940:
		1. Having fewer than 100 investors
		2. Ensuring all investors are accredited
- After the 2008 financial crisis, the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 required any hedge fund advisor managing more than $100 million in assets to register with the SEC.
