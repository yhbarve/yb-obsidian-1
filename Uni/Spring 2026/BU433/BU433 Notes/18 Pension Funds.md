# Ch 18: Pension Funds
---
### Pension Funds
- Pension funds are retirement savings accumulated from contributions of employers, employees, or both when individuals are still working.
- Funds are collectively managed and invested with influence from government regulations.
- The primary objective is to provide individuals who have reached retirement age with income in the form of a lifetime pension or capital.
- There are three ways of saving money for retirement:
	1. Government-funded plans like CPP or QPP
	2. Employer-sponsored pension plans
	3. Individual Savings Plans like RRSPs
- Pension funds traditionally focus more on risk to fulfill their fiduciary duty to retirees and are therefore less aggressive on returns.
	- Risk for pension funds is that their asset growth from investment returns and new contributions will not meet their liabilities to beneficiaries.
---
### Employer-Sponsored Pension Plans
- It's a private pension plan that is organized by a company that provides individuals with retirement income.
	- To qualify as tax sheltered, plans must be registered with the government.
	- Under these plans, both the employer and the employee contribute money.
	- When employees retire, they collect benefits from the plan.
	- Employees can also transfer their old pension to a new plan when they switch jobs and work with a different employer (if the benefits are vested i.e. employee has worked for enough years).
- Private pension plans can vary widely along different dimensions:
	- Contributory or non-contributory
	- Voluntary or compulsory
	- Trusteed or insured
- Pension plans can be divided into two types based on how retirement benefits are earned:
	1. Defined benefits plans
		- Employees use a specific formula to promise to pay employees a set amount of retirement income.
		- Both parties contribute to the plan, and the employers invest and manage the fund while employees do not involve themselves with any investment decisions.
		- The entire risk of investment and growing the money is with the employer.
		- Annual benefit is calculated with the formula: **2% x average salary from the last three years x number of years worked**.
			- When an employee joins, they only know the formula, but the actual dollar amount is calculated and finalized at the end of their career.
	2. Defined contribution plans
		- Employees know how much they contribute to the plan, but the benefits are unknown and depend on how the plan is managed and how the investments perform.
		- Employee contributions and the employer’s contributions are fixed, and the assets grow on a tax-deferred basis.
		- Employees can choose how the money is invested, but the options are limited, and the funds cannot be withdrawn before the owner retires.
		- the risk or the responsibility of growing the money lies entirely with the employee.
	- In the private sector, defined contribution plans have become more common.
	- Changes in the labor market and shifts in business practices have driven this trend.
	- Employers have largely shifted the risk to employees, but defined contribution plans still offer several clear advantages for employees.
		- DC plans are highly portable and fit the modern career path where people change jobs multiple times. In a traditional DB plan, your final payout heavily relies on staying with one company for a very long time.
		- Most DC plans operate on a matching system. This is essentially an immediate, guaranteed 100% return on your money before it is even invested.
		- In a DB plan, if the company goes bankrupt and the pension fund is severely underfunded, retirees may lose a portion of their promised lifetime benefits. With a DC plan, the money is held in an individual account under your name with an external financial institution, so your DC retirement fund remains completely safe and untouched.
		- Financial institutions know that most employees are not expert investors. Even though DC plans give you control, they almost always provide automated, hands-off options like a fund.
- A cash balance pension plan was developed in the U.S. as a response to the shortcomings of DB and DC plans. The benefit is a lump-sum amount determined by a formula, rather than a periodic contribution.

### Government Funded Pension Plans
-  Old Age Security (OAS) pension is one of the two pillars of Canada's retirement income system.
	- Old Age Security (OAS) is a monthly payment available to seniors aged 65 and older who have Canadian legal status and have lived in Canada for at least 10 years since age 18.
	- For seniors with lower income, the Guaranteed Income Supplement (GIS) is added to the monthly OAS payment.
- The other pillar is the Canadian Pension Plan (CPP) or the Quebec Pension Plan (QPP)
	-  CPP and QPP benefits are based on the time you contribute and your total lifetime contributions, as well as your pre-retirement income level.
	- Membership in both plans is mandatory for everyone in the labor force between the ages of 18 and 65.
	- The CPP pension is a taxable monthly benefit that replaces part of your income at retirement.
	- Once you are qualified, you will receive the benefit for the rest of your life.
	- CPP payments are not automatic, so you should apply six months before you want your pension to start, as early as age 60.
	- Taking CPP at age 60 means you will lose up to 36% of your pension because your benefit is reduced by 0.6% for each month you take it before your normal retirement age of 65.
	- Your benefit increases by 0.7% for each month after age 65, which works out to up to 8.5% per year.
	- There is no increase after age 70. Most people start receiving CPP the month after their 65th birthday.
	- The maximum pensionable earnings under the CPP for 2024 increased to $68,500. 
	- Contributors who earn more than $68,500 in 2024 are not required or permitted to make additional contributions to the CPP.
	- At age 70, a person stops contributing to CPP even if they are still working.

### Personal Savings Plan - RRSP
- Registered Retirement Savings Plans (RRSPs) are like pension plan contributions but completely managed by the individual.
	- RRSP is a tax-free savings plan used to invest for retirement 
- After age 71, the RRSP account must be converted to a registered retirement income fund (RRIF).
	- RRIF is a tax-sheltered account allowing individuals to withdraw income after retirement
- RRSPs are registered with the Canada Revenue Agency, which determines the maximum annual contribution limit for every individual taxpayer.
	- Contribution limits are registered with other employer-sponsored registered pension plans.
- All Canadians are eligible to purchase RRSPs regardless of age
