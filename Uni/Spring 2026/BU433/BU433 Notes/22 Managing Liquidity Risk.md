# Liquidity Risk Management & Causes
- Unlike other risks, liquidity risk is a normal aspect of the everyday management of an FI.
- At the extreme, liquidity risk can lead to insolvency.
- Liquidity risk arises for two reasons:
	1. **Liability-side reason** occurs when FI's liability holders seek to cash in their financial claims immediately. FIs must meet withdrawals by borrowing additional funds or liquidating assets.
	2. **Asset-side reason** arises when FI needs to fund loans immediately. Loan commitment allows a customer to borrow funds from an FI on demand, and when a borrower draws on its loan commitment, the FI must fund the loan on the balance sheet immediately.

# Liquidity Risk and Depository Institutions
- DIs' balance sheets typically have large amounts of short-term liabilities, such as demand deposits and other transaction accounts, that fund relatively long-term illiquid assets.
- Demand deposit accounts and other transaction accounts are contracts that give holders the right to put their financial claims back to the DI on any given day and demand immediate repayment of the face value in cash.
- DI managers monitor net deposit drains, which is the amount by which cash withdrawals exceed additions.
- Financial institutions manage or drain deposits in two major ways:
	1. **Purchased Liquidity Management** is an adjustment to a deposit drain that occurs on the liability side of the balance sheet. DI manager utilizes the markets for purchased funds, which are interbank markets for short-term loans.
	2. **Stored Liquidity Management** is an adjustment to a deposit drain that occurs on the asset side of the balance sheet. The financial institution liquidates some of its assets, utilizing stored liquidity.
- Just as deposit drains can cause a DI liquidity problem, so can loan requests.
- DIs increase their loan commitments tremendously in the recent years with the belief that they would not be exercised.
- - -
# Financing gap and the financing requirement
- One way to measure liquidity risk exposure is to determine the DI's financing gap. It is the difference between a DI's average loans and average deposits. $$ \text{Financing Gap} = \text{Average Loans} - \text{Average Deposits} $$
- If the financing gap is positive, the DI must find liquidity to fill the gap. Funding may come via either purchase liquidity management or store liquidity management.
- **Financing requirement** is the financing gap plus our liquid assets. $$ \text{Financing Gap} + \text{Liquid Assets} = \text{Financing Requirement} $$

# Sources and uses of liquidity
- Net Liquidity Statement lists the sources and uses of liquidity and thus provides a measure of a DI's net liquidity position.
- DI can obtain liquid funds in three ways:
	1. Sell its liquid assets immediately with little price risk and low transaction costs.
	2. Borrow funds in the money or purchased funds market up to a maximum amount.
	3. Use any excess cash reserves over and above the amount held to meet regulatory-imposed reserve requirements.

# Pure Group Ratio Comparisons
- This is the third way to measure a DI's liquidity exposure. Compare certain of its key ratios and balance sheet features with those for DI's of a similar size and geographic location.

# Liquidity index
- This is the final way to measure liquidity risk. A liquidity index measures potential losses a DI could suffer as a result of a sudden disposal of assets.
- The larger the differences between fire sale asset prices and fair market prices, the less liquid the D.I.'s asset portfolio
- Liquidity index will always lie between 0 and 1.
- Liquidity index for a particular DI could also be compared, where the index is calculated for a peer group.
$$ I = \sum_{i = 1}^{N}[(w_i)(\frac{P_i}{P^{*}_i})]$$
- where:
	- $w_i=$ Percentage of each asset in the DIS portfolio ($\sum w_i = 1$)
	- $P_i=$ Price if a DI liquidates asset $i$ today
	- $P^*_i=$ Price if a DI liquidates asset $i$ at some future point in time

# New Liquidity Risk Measures
- Two regulatory standards were developed by BIS for liquidity risk supervision.
1. **Liquidity coverage ratio** aims to ensure that a DI maintains an adequate level of high-quality liquid assets that can be converted into cash to meet liquidity needs for a 30-day time horizon. $$ LCR = \frac{\text{Stock of high-quality liquid assets }}{\text{Total net cash outflows over the next 30 days }} \geq 100\%$$
2. **Net Stable Funds Ratio** takes a longer-term look at liquidity on a DIS balance sheet. $$ \text{Net stable funding ratio} = \frac{\text{Available amount of stable funding }}{\text{Required amount of stable funding }} \geq 100$$

# Liquidity planning
- Liquidity planning allows managers to make important borrowing priority decisions before liquidity problems arise.
- Liquidity plans have a number of components:
	- Delineation of managerial details and responsibilities
	- Detailed list of fund providers most likely to withdraw funds and a pattern of fund withdrawals
	- Identification of the size of potential deposit and fund withdrawals over various time horizons in the future
	- Internal limits on separate subsidiaries' and branches' borrowings, as well as acceptable risk premiums to pay in each market

# Unexpected Deposit Drains and Bank Runs
- Major liquidity problems arise if deposit drains are abnormally large and unexpected.
- Abnormal drain stains can occur for a number of reasons:
	- Concerns about a DI solvency relative to that of other DIs
	- Failure of a related DI leading to a heightened deposit of concerns about the solvency of surviving DIs (contagion effect)
	- Sudden changes in investor preferences regarding holding non-bank financial assets relative to DI deposits
- A **bank run** is a sudden and unexpected increase in deposit withdrawals from a DI.
- Demand deposits are first-come, first-served contracts in the sense that the order in which depositors place their deposits determines the amount he or she will be able to withdraw from a DI.
- Incentives for depositors to withdraw their funds at the first sign of trouble create a fundamental instability in the banking system.
- A **bank panic** is a systemic or contagious run on the deposits of the banking industry as a whole.
- Regulatory mechanisms are in place to ease banks' liquidity problems and to deter bank runs and panics.
	- Deposit insurance
	- Discount window
- - -
# Deposit Insurance
- FDIC was created in 1933 in the wake of the banking panics when some 10,000 commercial banks failed.
- Deposit Insurance was first introduced with a coverage of up to $2,500.
- Coverage was increased to $100,000 in 1980 and to $250,000 in October.
- Individuals can achieve many times the $250,000 coverage cap on deposits by structuring their deposits in a particular fashion.
- The primary intention of deposit insurance is to deter DI runs and panics, but a secondary and related objective has been to protect the smaller and less informed saver against the reduction in wealth that would occur if that person were last in line if a DI was to fail.
- - -
# Discount window 
- The Fed also provides a discount window lending facility, serving as a lender of last resort.
- Interest rate at which securities are discounted is called the **discount rate** and is set by the central bank.

# Liquidity Risk and Insurance Companies
- Life insurance companies hold cash reserves and other liquid assets for the following reasons:
	- Make Policy Payments
	- Meet cancellation or surrender payments.
		- Surrender value of a life insurance policy is the amount that the insurance policy holder receives when cashing in a policy early.
	- Other working capital needs
- Property and casualty insurance companies' claims are relatively short-term and unpredictable, because of which they have a greater need for liquidity.
- - -
# Guarantee programs
- Both life and property and casualty insurance companies are regulated at the state level.
- Most states began to sponsor state guarantee funds for firms selling insurance in that state.
- It differs from Deposit Insurance in several important ways:
	- These programs are run and administered by the private insurance companies themselves.
	- No permanent guarantee fund exists for the insurance industry, with the sole exception of the state of New York.
	- Size of required contributions that surviving injurers make may vary widely from state to state.
	- Delay usually occurs before cash surrender values or other payment obligations are received from the Guarantee Fund. (**waiting period**)
- - -
# Liquidity risk and investment funds
- Investment funds, such as mutual funds and hedge funds, sell shares as liabilities to investors and invest the proceeds in assets such as bonds and equities.
- They face similar liquidity problems as DIs when the number of withdrawals rises to abnormally high or unexpected levels.
- There is a fundamental difference in the way investment fund contracts are valued compared to the valuation of DI deposit contracts. This reduces the incentives for investment fund shareholders to engage in deposit-like runs.
